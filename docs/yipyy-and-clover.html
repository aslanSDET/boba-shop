Yipyy and Clover

What this product actually is, who pays whom, and why a pet-care booking system contains twenty modules of payments code.

The product

Yipyy is software a pet business runs on

Not a booking widget — the whole operation. Yipyy Inc sells it to dog daycares, boarding kennels, grooming salons, training schools, pet retailers and vets. Each business that buys it is called a facility in the code, and the platform is multi-tenant: one deployment, many facilities, kept apart by row-level security in Postgres.

The pitch is consolidation. A grooming salon might otherwise run booking in one tool, payroll in another, a card terminal from a third and a loyalty punch-card on paper. Yipyy tries to be the single place all of that happens.

What a pet owner does

Books daycare or a groom, manages their pets' records and vaccinations, signs waivers electronically, completes check-in before arrival via a QR code, pays invoices, buys gift cards, earns loyalty points, and reads a "report card" describing how their dog's day went.

What the business does

Checks pets in and out, assigns kennels and rooms, logs care through the day, rings up retail on a counter terminal, rosters staff, runs the time clock and payroll, sets pricing, chases lapsed clients, and — for multi-site groups — compares locations from an HQ view.

Four kinds of person use it, and which one you are decides which portal you land in: customers (/customer), facility admins (/facility/dashboard), staff (/employee, scoped by permission), and Yipyy's own platform team (/dashboard). The address matters too: www.yipyy.com is Yipyy's console, while <slug>.yipyy.com is one facility's front door for its own staff and customers.

The other half

Clover is how the money actually moves

Clover is a payments company owned by Fiserv. You have seen their hardware without necessarily knowing the name — the white countertop terminals and card readers at small independent businesses. They do two things that matter here: they sell those terminals, and they act as the acquirer, the institution that processes a card transaction and settles the money into the merchant's bank account.

So Yipyy is the software; Clover is the money. The reason this repo carries so much payments code is that connecting the two is not a single API call — it is an onboarding process, a consent handshake, two different charge paths, and a reconciliation problem.

That missing arrow drives a real product decision. Since Yipyy cannot ask Clover what has settled, it nets its own recorded card payments and reversals to work out what should land, then labels the figure an estimate on every screen that shows it, with a link to Clover for the authoritative number.

Clover publishes no payout or settlement endpoint to an OAuth application… So the choice is between showing nothing and showing a number Yipyy can actually justify.

"Powered by Clover" appears only where the facility is genuinely dealing with the processor — the landing hero, the dashboard header, and either side of the redirect to Clover's consent screen. What matters technically is the boundary this creates around what the product may claim, because Clover is an acquirer rather than a platform:

Every field the product exposes is either something Yipyy genuinely owns, or something the facility is declaring so Yipyy can compute with it. Nothing in this domain commands Clover, and no screen is allowed to imply otherwise.

Reading the repo

Where to look, and one doc not to trust

If the payments integration is why this repo was handed to you, the relevant surface is small and well-commented:

  • src/lib/clover/ — 20 modules: OAuth, charge, refund, reversal, terminals, devices, receipts, reconciliation, payouts
  • src/app/api/payments/ — 23 route handlers, the online and terminal charge paths, and the retail counter
  • src/app/api/webhooks/clover — how the ledger corrects itself when someone refunds inside Clover's own dashboard
  • payments, payment_intents, payment_connections, payment_webhook_events — the schema behind all of it

The comments in these files are worth reading properly. Several record things established by probing Clover's live sandbox because the published documentation covers a different flow — for instance that a merchant's public key lives on a third host entirely, and the same path on the main API host returns a bare 404.

One document will actively mislead you. docs/product/overview.md describes Yipyy as "a mock-driven prototype" where "data is in-memory", "real payments are mocked", and Anthropic is "the only live integration."

That was true once and is now wrong: 188 of 216 API routes query Postgres, there are 214 migrations and 462 row-level-security policies, and the Clover integration runs against a real merchant account. AGENTS.md and CLAUDE.md are current — that product doc was never updated after the backend landed.
